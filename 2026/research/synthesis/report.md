## 0. The principles this report applies

Section 0b is immersive theatre, larp and asymmetric co-op (`data/hw-research-immersive/report.md`, 26 sources; the S numbers are that report's). Each rule names the source that backs it.

### 0a. Escape-room craft

Rules a builder can apply, each with the sources behind it.

#### Structure

1. Pick the structure by team size and story need. Sequential suits a small team that stays together and puzzles that need everyone; path-based lets a team split and lets difficulty ramp; open suits large groups and is hardest to pace. Most real rooms are hybrids. (Nicholson 2015, 2.1; Wiemker 2.4.)
2. Any linear stretch must be short and easy, and no puzzle on the critical path may be workable by one person only. "If you need to make some part linear, use very easy puzzles." (Matoušek 2.16; Wiemker 2.4; Elumir rule 7, 2.5.)
3. Use branching to keep all hands busy and chokepoints to move the story. End with a linear finish "to ensure everyone is watching the final moments." (Matoušek 2.16; Logic Escapes Me 2.15.)
4. The finale is a meta-puzzle: its answer is assembled from what earlier puzzles produced, and the pieces should be physical. (Wiemker and Clare 2.4; Nicholson's pyramid 2.1.)
5. Separate information from where it is used, and give each player a prop or ability that makes them necessary. (Short 2.14; Wiemker 2.4 on puzzles that need several people.)
6. The dependency graph must be acyclic, each clue is used once, and nothing big can be missed. (Matoušek 2.16; Spira dos and don'ts 2.13.)

#### Objective legibility

7. Ask why of every object and task. The player needs "a meaningful reason for taking on a task other than 'it's the next thing to do in the room'." (Nicholson Ask Why 2.3.)
8. Actions must be discernable and integrated: players see the result of what they did, and it changes the game. Puzzles give feedback and answers self-validate. (Nicholson 2.3; Elumir rules 4 and 10, 2.5.)
9. Players must be able to tell which gates are open and which clue belongs to which gate. (Elumir 2.5 rule 5, 2.6.)
10. Rules are stated once, clearly and in the fiction, and never violated by the game. (Spira 2.13; the earlier finale research's ARGNet note on The Man From Beyond.)
11. A clock can be diegetic if it is legible: the zombie's chain lengthens every five minutes. Solution-triggered reveals are easier to stage than timer-triggered ones. (Nicholson 2.1; Short 2.14.)
12. Players' focus narrows under a clock; plan where their attention will be and direct it. (Hall 2.17.)

#### Hints

13. The hint system is part of the design, not an afterthought. A hint request means the fun has stopped; hint rate per puzzle is a tuning metric. (Spira 2.7.)
14. Most players will not ask. A gamemaster watching and intervening on their own judgment beats request-only, and open-line nudging reduces time stuck. (Nicholson 2.1; Spira 2.7; Short 2.14.)
15. Caps of two or three hints are arbitrary and push players to suffer rather than ask. Operators who dropped caps did not regret it. (Spira 2.7; Suleski and Breakout 2.18.)
16. Time hints so you never take the breakthrough away. Operators use milestone times and a short wait before stepping in. (Nicholson 2.1; Live Escape, unverified, 2 end.)
17. Size hints from a single-word pointer upward, and run two channels: a subtle one and a tangible one. (Logic Escapes Me 2.15.)
18. Hints in character "don't feel like hints." Narrator and optional-reception styles let players take a hint without admitting defeat. Make the hint system part of the theme. (Burns 2.8, 2.9; Matoušek 2.16.)
19. An in-room actor must build trust fast, play to the players, read the quiet ones, keep monologues short, and use their power with restraint. (Spira 2.10.)

#### Pacing and reveals

20. Keep players between frustration and boredom; release new content on a regular beat. (Wiemker 2.4; Short 2.14.)
21. No puzzle over five minutes for a player who knows the method; add puzzles, not tedium. Too many aha puzzles shut out newcomers. (Elumir 2.5, 2.6.)
22. Every puzzle is a standalone memorable moment; vary types; cut the throwaways. (Spira 2.13.)
23. Before a reveal, bottleneck the group so everyone is looking at the same place, then cue with sound or light. (Short 2.14; Matoušek 2.16 on chokepoints for story points.)
24. Story reveals should change play: a challenge that yields a tool, a new encoding, or a new understanding is more meaningful than one that yields a code. (Nicholson 2.3; Nicholson 2.2 on puzzles that move the narrative.)
25. Start shallow and deepen; players confused by the setting at minute one stay confused. (Nicholson 2.3.)
26. After the climax, show evidence the world changed and leave space before the host breaks immersion. (Burns 2.12.)

#### Fail states

27. Design the failure ending as a set piece as exciting as the win. "Meaningful stakes equals meaningful failure." (Stein 2.11; Clare via Short 2.14.)
28. Tier the win: a core objective most groups reach, harder objectives for a better ending. Avoid instant fail with time left. (Stein 2.11.)
29. Most facilities walk a failed team through the rest and debrief; players should leave knowing what they missed. (Nicholson 2.1; Spira dos and don'ts 2.13.)
30. Full-escape rates around 40 percent are normal for commercial rooms; hosts tune that live through hints. (Nicholson 2.1; Breakout 2.18.)

#### Practicality

31. No prop whose failure ends the game; map the critical path and plan a bypass for each prop; be ready to cover in character. (Logic Escapes Me 2.15.)
32. Check the set before every group. Props will be broken. No destroyable puzzle states. (Spira 2.13; Hall 2.17; Elumir 2.5.)
33. A home room can run 15-minute slots with 30-second resets if each room's props live in one bin and nothing has to be re-hidden. (Lock Paper Scissors 2.19.)
34. Fog: keep visibility at 2 m or more, never aim at floors, 50 cm from the nozzle, expect smoke alarms. In a dark room give a light or a sound to navigate by. Always a way out. (Manual 2.20; Matoušek 2.16.)
35. Blindfolds: a sighted guide owns the blind player's safety, the route is walked in advance, the blindfold can come off at any time. (Trust-walk guides 2.21; Matoušek 2.16 on never making players do unsafe things.)
36. "An escape room is only as good as the people running it." One actor in the room plus one person on effects is the published working model; the actor must know every puzzle cold. (Lock Paper Scissors 2.19; Burns 2.9; Nicholson 2.1 on in-room actors in 10 percent of rooms.)

#### Mistakes experienced designers name

- Puzzles too hard; nobody tests with newcomers. (Elumir rule 12.)
- Difficulty faked with tedium: more screws, more searching, more counting. (Elumir 2.6.)
- Red herrings. (Elumir, Matoušek, a Nicholson respondent.)
- Ambiguity about which clue goes where, and locks that become the puzzle. (Elumir 2.5, 2.6.)
- Objects with no story reason, "escape room logic." (Nicholson 2.3.)
- Flimsy builds, repeated lock types, throwaway puzzles. (Spira 2.13.)
- Long monologues from actors; actors who do not read the whole team. (Spira 2.10.)
- An ending that is a host saying "time's up," and a story that stops at the climax. (Stein 2.11; Burns 2.12.)
- A non-linear room where several puzzles are partly available but none can be finished, so players "cannot tell what is relevant." (Room Escape Artist's Trapped Room 9 review, seen only in a search summary.)
- Hidden hint caps and hint penalties that stop players asking. (Nicholson 2.1; Suleski 2.18.)

### 0b. Immersive theatre, larp and asymmetric cooperation

These are design rules synthesized from the sources, not universal laws or decisions for this game.

1. **Specify the audience's job.** Exploration is a valid theatrical activity, but a cooperative game also needs an actionable shared goal. State what to do and what counts as finished. [S1](https://www.punchdrunk.com/work/sleep-no-more-new-york/), [S19](https://www.bombmanual.com/web/index.html).
2. **Teach participation before raising its cost.** Rehearse the smallest version of an unfamiliar action, then alter its conditions. Do not ask novices to learn a new communication system during the loudest reveal. [S3](https://strangebirdimmersive.com/immersology/iterating-interactions/), [S21](https://sitdown-games.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/MM_Rules_EN_HR_Sept2018_LD.pdf).
3. **Give revelations an audience.** Converge active work before essential information. Do not reveal the next attractive task until the group has received the current scene. [S7](https://strangebirdimmersive.com/immersology/bottlenecks/).
4. **Balance exchanges, not word counts.** A role's information should prompt useful interaction soon. A whole page for one player and a button for another is not inherently balanced. Time the back-and-forth with players. [S20](https://www.reddit.com/r/pcgaming/comments/ebhrd3/we_are_steel_crate_games_creators_of_keep_talking/).
5. **Keep an available path through each restriction.** Hidden information, silence, and lost sight work when the remaining actions are legible. Audit who can receive, interpret, transmit, and act on each necessary fact. [S22](https://www.rnrgames.com/Content/RRGames/images/ProductRules/hanabiRules.PDF), [S24](https://www.64ouncegames.com/pages/nyctophobia).
6. **Separate fear peaks from difficult interpretation.** Reduce competing effects when players need to understand an instruction. Build intensity back once they know what to do. [S10](https://numberoneescaperoom.com/how-to-build-a-horror-escape-room-a-designers-complete-guide/).
7. **Make the fiction enforceable by the players.** Define a trigger and a specific response. “Be possessed” needs translation into permitted behavior. [S16](https://www.nordiclarp.org/2024/06/25/the-interaction-engine/).
8. **Give the performer controllable work.** Automate repeatable coordination; reserve live judgment for attention, hints, readiness, and distress. This is an adaptation of actor responsiveness and grouped cueing, not proof that solo operation is safe. [S8](https://strangebirdimmersive.com/immersology/100-seances-later/), [S26](https://qlab.app/docs/v5/fundamentals/group-cues/).
9. **Make progress and deadline independently readable.** A solved state should persist. Escalation should not reverse that signal accidentally. Distinct module status and timer displays in KTANE supply the precedent. [S19](https://www.bombmanual.com/web/index.html).
10. **Design defeat as an ending.** The team can lose its fictional objective while receiving a complete performance and a humane return to the party. [S9](https://strangebirdimmersive.com/immersology/bookends-fulfilling-finales/), [S18](https://lizziestark.com/2012/07/15/how-debrief-freeform-game/comment-page-1/).
11. **Safety overrides the fiction in every role.** A silence or hearing rule cannot remove the ability to call or receive a stop. Test the stop with the actual effects running. [S17](https://www.nordiclarp.org/2016/09/09/creating-culture-trust-safety-calibration-larp-mechanics/), [S13](https://tdi.texas.gov/fire/haunted-house-builders-operators.html).


## 1. Where the design stands tonight

The evening confirmed the skeleton. Hub-and-spoke is the right structure for four people who stay together, and the handoff's instincts (one objective sentence, the fourth relic as the trigger, the soundtrack as the clock, self-applied curses, no arbitrary codes, the blind player seated) each match a named rule in the escape-room and immersive-theatre sources. All four finale directions can be made to run: each playtest reached a win and a staged loss with four to eleven added rules and no new story beyond the handoff.

It broke two things. Time: the investigation as written runs 20 to 23 minutes before the turn in every playtest, the curses then eat 2:30 of the soundtrack, and a winning run is 25 to 29 minutes against a 20-minute target. The first half as a game: in every room one person works while three watch, the hint channel never lands, the return to the table does nothing, doors are chosen for no reason in the fiction, the truth at the fourth relic is announced rather than assembled, and the deaf player loses the objective at the moment it is given. Two of the handoff's own floated finale props, the whispered box and the spoken testimony, are unplayable under its own curses without a non-verbal channel.

It left open the three finale sentences, which are the captain's, now with four tested candidates. New since the handoff: when the clock starts, how long the evening is, whether the husband's truth must be the win, what three of four means, and how each spoke plants what its player will later have to tell with a faculty gone.

## 2. The three finale questions: the four directions compared

The proposals, verbatim from each playtest's section 1.

**Resurrection** (`hw-play-resurrection`, PROPOSAL):
- "She now intends to put each of her four dead children into the living guest who carries that child's mark, one child at a time, in the order she called them."
- "If the players fail, the fire ends with the mother calling her children's names and the guests answering from where they sit: each guest she took goes still, their mark goes dark, and their child's scream in the fire has become a voice answering her; the medium ends the seance to release the guests. With fewer than four taken, the ending names which children she has and which are safe."
- "The players win when they cut each child's hold on its bearer before that child's scream reaches them: the bearer, who has lost the faculty, tells the way, and another guest who still has that faculty does the act; the fourth cut, before the fourth scream, ends the ritual."

**Rage** (`hw-play-rage`, PROPOSAL):
- "She now intends to put her husband into the fire: to condemn him as he condemned her, and let the haunting burn him as it burned the children."
- "If the players fail, the fire reaches him: his voice joins the children's screams, then the soundscape cuts to silence, the lights drop, and the medium closes the circle with six spirits unresolved."
- "The players win when they assemble the four parts of his reason from the four relics on the table and one of them says it aloud to her before the fire reaches him."

**Loop** (`hw-play-loop`, PROPOSAL):
- "She now intends to force the house through the children's fatal fire again."
- "If the players fail, the fire reaches its fatal ending and repeats its opening sound and light once; the medium declares the loop unbroken, raises usable light, and ends play before anyone moves."
- "The players win when they show and witness the four recovered accounts beside the matching keepsakes, then speak the agreed account at the table before the fatal ending."

**Rescue** (`hw-play-rescue`, PROPOSAL):
- "She now intends to hold her children within the recreated fire."
- "If the players fail, the unrescued children's keepsakes remain in the fire-marked center as the fire reaches its endpoint; the medium names how many remain caught, then ends the séance."
- "The players win when they transfer all four keepsakes to the safe edge of the séance table and the seated receiver accepts the fourth before the fire ends."

**Ranking, a recommendation the captain can overrule.**

1. **Rage.** The only tested direction where the last revelation is the win, which the handoff asks for in section 12 and principles 3 and 11, and which the immersive report calls the line between a finale and an epilogue. Its last act is one spoken thing everyone watches, its loss is a set piece, and it fits the turn's proposed state, in which she blames the husband. Its three strains are designer-level and two are shared with resurrection: the clock start, Remember's stake, the spokes planting what the finale needs. The content it needs, his reason in four parts, is already one sentence in the handoff's section 3.
2. **Resurrection.** The best fit to the fiction (four dead, four living, four senses; it explains the marks) and the best use of the curses, since each bearer tells what only they know. Its 15-second trap disappears with one captain call on the clock. But the husband's truth is absent, and making it matter would need a rule nobody tested. Pick this if the husband's truth may be told rather than played.
3. **Rescue.** The clearest physical act and the only one where the blind player's part is tactile and essential. But it is the one direction proven bypassable by three players, it has no revelation, and the safe edge is a boundary the fiction asserts. The handoff already doubted what rescue means for dead children; the playtest did not resolve that.
4. **Loop.** It needed a cause the story does not have and repeats the fourth-relic reveal as a ritual. The author's own verdict is to reject it unless an unfinished act already in the agreed history can be found.

Resurrection and rage independently point at the same three moves: start the clock after the fourth curse, make each spoke plant its bearer's finale knowledge, give Remember judgment rather than custody. They are carried in section 7, not blended into a fifth direction.

## 3. What the playtests found in the first half

All four Part 1 sections agree. The investigation engine was unchanged in every run.

- **Three watch while one works.** Every room ran as four people reading for twenty seconds and one working for one to four minutes. Rage counted about 9:30 of 18:17 per watcher; resurrection's Waitstill room gave three players nothing for 3:52; in rescue one player did another child's whole spoke. Fix: the escape report's section 4.1, every spoke workable by all four with the information in one place and the thing to manipulate in another; the immersive report's rehearsal count of who contributes; rage's proposal that one spoke or the opening gate needs two people on two things, so splitting work is learned before the finale demands it.
- **The hint channel.** The escape report found no channel in the fiction, since neither the medium nor the mother knows the history. The engine's one channel, an offer after sixty seconds of effort, never met a human asking when confused. Rage: zero hints in 22 minutes, three refusals. Resurrection: hints arrived as the step was about to fall and saved at most fifteen seconds; two of six paid. Rescue and loop: refused at zero effort, and the hint carries no reason. Fix: the escape report's section 4.5 channels (the medium reads the table, not the puzzle; a one-word pointer from the spirit by raps or a card; an optional face-down card per child) with the "obey" command as the bypass tier, offered on confusion, at about 90 seconds, three minutes, and a hard bypass at four.
- **Nobody knows which door, and the return does nothing.** At every table the players chose a door by the order the children were named, and each return was a 30-second corridor that changed only the relic list. Fix: the escape report's items 4.1 and 4.3.7, placing a relic visibly does something and the medium only sends the group out again after it lands, naming the next door; the immersive report's "make each return an advance", so her belief narrows each time.
- **The turn is told, not found.** The fourth relic completes no deduction; the engine declares that all four died. In rage's split play each player had seen only their own child's reveal; rescue notes that "part of what happened" supports no inference. Fix: the ledger's question 6 on reveal pacing, a captain call in section 5; the immersive requirement that the fourth return completes a conclusion the players can state.
- **The deaf player loses the objective.** The objective is spoken after the fourth curse, and the muffled player hears nothing from their own curse on: no later invocations, no objective, no hints. Rescue notes the engine leaks a bookkeeping line to that player; no report claims the fiction delivers it. Loop and rescue fixed it with a printed objective and persistent visible state; resurrection and rage put her progress on light as well as sound. Fix: every finale instruction and progress cue has a visible form, or the Mindwell curse is a rule against using the spirit's audio clues rather than real muffling, which the immersive report prefers for a first prototype.
- **The 15-second first window.** The soundtrack starts at the fourth relic and the first free action comes 2:30 later, so ten minutes is 7:30 of play. Resurrection's first child was due 1:30 after the last curse: fifteen seconds of margin for a group that does everything right, and one confer lost a child. Rage's slow group lost by about twenty seconds and won with 1:25 to spare when the clock started after the curses. Fix: engine question 4, a captain call in section 5.
- **Splitting.** Nothing in the opening says to stay together. The group that split reached the fourth curse at 9:36 but lost every hint, every shared reveal, and nearly fired the turn with a player away. The ledger records four-together as decided (W2), which answers engine question 1; the fiction must say it, through the medium sending them out as one.

The sim's sixteen minutes are puzzle effort 8:00, reading 2:40, walking 2:40, reveals 2:00, placing 0:40. To land twenty minutes with a ten-minute soundtrack, opening, four rooms and turn must fit in ten, about 1:50 per room including the walk and the reveal. The immersive report says the same the other way: three quarters investigation plus a ten-minute finale is a forty-minute evening.

The sim's sensitivity, from the 20:30 baseline to the end of the curses:
- One step per spoke instead of two: 15:10. The biggest lever, and the escape report's independent conclusion: one gate per spoke, not a chain.
- Travel 20 seconds to zero: 17:50. Adjacent rooms buy 2:40 without cutting puzzles.
- Splitting the group: 9:20, but it breaks W2 and the hint channel.
- A third step, or 90-second steps: 24:30 to 25:50.

The escape report's three pacing options, its own arithmetic from the sources' per-step caps:

|---|---|---|---|
| Opening with tutorial gate | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| Spokes | 3, 3, 3, 3 | 4, 3, 2.5, 2.5 | 4, 4 |

A and B keep the settled rhythm and assume a one-minute turn; the playtests measured 2:30 under the handoff's invocation beat. C halves the returns but breaks "one child, come back, repeat"; the escape author lists it only because it lands under 20, and the loop author says to keep the route and shorten the spoke instead. The playtests won in 3:17 to 5:00 of finale play, which supports a four-to-six-minute finale over the handoff's ten.

The one question only the captain can answer: how long is the evening, and how long is the soundtrack? With five groups and resets of up to 15 minutes (ledger W6, W7), 20-minute runs make about 2:40 of sittings and resets; 30-minute runs about 3:30. If 20 stands, the consequences are mechanical: one gate per spoke, spokes adjacent to the hub, the clock starting after the fourth curse, a soundtrack of six to seven minutes. If the ten-minute soundtrack stays, the run is 25 to 30 and the cap is the real target.

## 5. Captain's calls, in dependency order

Merged from the ledger's "answer these first", the engine's questions 1 to 8 and the playtests. Dropped as answered tonight: engine question 1 (the group stays together, W2), questions 6 and 7 (every playtest supplied a designer-level non-verbal channel, section 7), question 8 (subsumed by the winning act), and questions 9 to 12 (the directions themselves). Engine questions 2 and 3 are designer-level and sit in section 7.

**Needed before any puzzle design starts**

1. **Which direction?** She now intends to ______. Options: the four PROPOSALS in section 2, each carrying its failure state and winning act. Recommend rage; resurrection if the husband's truth may be told rather than played.
2. **Does the husband's reason unlock the win, or is it only told?** Options: gates the win; epilogue; absent. Recommend gates the win: the handoff asks for it and only rage passes. Answering this first effectively answers 1.
3. **How long is the evening, and how long the soundtrack?** Options: 20 minutes with a six-to-seven-minute soundtrack and one gate per spoke; 25; 30 with the ten-minute soundtrack. Recommend deciding the total first and letting section 4 set the rest; the evidence favours a four-to-six-minute finale.
4. **Does the clock start at the fourth relic or after the fourth curse?** Options: at the relic, 7:30 of play; after the curses, 10:00; at the relic with the first threat at least two minutes after the last curse. Recommend after the fourth curse: it is the difference between a win and a loss in two playtests and costs the fiction nothing.
5. **What does three of four mean?** Options: a loss with the count named, every playtest's rule; a tiered win, the escape report's Stein; a full win only. Recommend a tiered ending staged as its own beat: all four playtests already report the count, and the sources say failure must be a set piece.
6. **Do spokes one to three each reveal that child's death, or does only the fourth prove it?** Options: partial fate per spoke so the fourth completes a pattern; roles only until the fourth. Recommend partial per spoke: without it the turn is announced, not found, in every run.
7. **How did each child die, and how dark may the spokes and the fire be?** Captain's taste; no evidence to weigh. Needed before any fate content or scream in the soundscape.
8. **Does failing or stalling in a spoke cost anything before the turn?** Options: nothing but time; a curse or offering; a person. Recommend nothing but time, with the hard bypass at four minutes as the real cost: linear sections cannot absorb a stall and a missed early action must not delete the finale.
9. **Does anyone leave before the end, and can the group lose members in the finale?** Options: nobody leaves and a taken player keeps playing; removal. Recommend nobody leaves: all four playtests kept every player in, and the immersive report forbids a penalty for opting out of a curse.
10. **Are the Informer and the healer in this version?** Recommend out: the handoff never mentions them, its betrayer is a neighbor, and all four directions ran without them.
11. **Is the mother a live performer, and who, or a voice? What is the captain's role on the night?** Options: Tamari only; a second live performer; recorded or offstage voice with the captain on cues and effects. Recommend the voice with the captain on cues: one medium cannot also perform the mother and run effects, and two directions need her to speak marks on the clock, which a recording does reliably.

**Can wait, but each blocks something named**

12. **Which room is the hub and which four are the spokes?** Blocks placement and the fire survey, not puzzle forms. An afternoon's walk.
13. **Is the first curse at the turn, or is someone blinded earlier?** Recommend at the turn, with the "obey" rule taught once at the séance as a tutorial gate instead.
14. **Is which player gets which child decided by chance or by the medium?** Recommend the medium, with consent: never impose a restriction a guest cannot use.
