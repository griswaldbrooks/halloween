# Finale and win/lose design for the Halloween 2026 game

Scout report, 2026-10-01. Task `halloween-finale-research`. Web research only. I changed nothing in the halloween repo.

## Short answer

Build the finale as a roll call. The mother calls her four children home by name, one at a time, against the sand timer. The players answer for each child. They put that child's keepsake on the child's name plate and say out loud what really happened to the child. Her version of the story does nothing. The truth puts the child to rest and frees the player tied to that child. When the sand runs out she takes whoever is left, by name, by the hand. Zero to four players walk out, and nobody knows the number until the sand is gone.

If players are taken during the game, seat them in four small chairs in the last room and let the survivors win them back with the same rite. That is the same finale with some players sitting down.

Idea three on the board, matching keepsakes to graves, is most of the way there. Section 2 says what I think it is missing. Section 5 has five worked proposals and section 6 ranks them.

## 1. What I did

I read the design board at `data/halloween-board/index.html`, mainly the Tonight section on line 62, plus the planning notes in `data/halloween-2026-notes.md`. The constraints I designed against come from those two files.

- The finale is the open problem. A tally is "not a finale", the surprise return of removed players is "clunky", and keepsakes on graves is "closer, still missing something". Board line 62.
- Survivor count must not be guaranteed, and the captain wants "an element of chance in how many are left". Notes line 116.
- The bar is fun and creepy, and the ending has to make sense to players. Notes line 118.

- Removed players coming back as her children is liked but feared too complex. Board line 43, notes line 91.
- Whether removed players go back to the party is a draft assumption that nobody approved. Board line 36.

Then I ran about 50 web searches and opened about 40 pages. I read Scott Nicholson's two escape room papers in full as PDFs. Section 8 lists every source and says whether I opened the page myself or only saw a search summary of it. Where I could not confirm something, the text says so.

## 2. What the keepsakes and graves idea is missing

I think it is missing five things. Each one shows up in the research as a known way finales go flat.

The mother has nothing to do. Sorting keepsakes onto graves is a quiet puzzle, and the main character of the night stands outside it. Room Escape Artist made the same complaint about The Hex Room, where six players start in separate cells and then come together. Their [review](https://roomescapeartist.com/2016/11/15/cross-roads-escape-games-the-hex-room-review/) says "once our team came together, the game wasn't able to do anything with the anticipation or build to a satisfying climax".

The result is settled before the finale starts. A keepsake handed over in minute ten decides a death in minute twenty five. Nothing the group does in the last five minutes changes it. That is the rejected tally again, with props.

The truth has no use. Players spend the night finding out that she sent her own children out as couriers. Then the last puzzle does not need that fact.

The Informer has no part in it.

Nothing happens after it. Richard Burns at Room Escape Artist [argues](https://roomescapeartist.com/2021/10/09/untie-escape-room-stories) that many escape rooms stop dead at the climax, so players never see what their actions did.

So keep the graves. Put her in the room as the opponent, run it on the clock, make the truth the key, and leave the count open until the sand runs out.

## 3. Twelve finale patterns

### 3.1 What the players learned is the weapon

Undertale's last fight cannot be won by attacking. In the [Asriel battle](https://undertale.wiki/w/Asriel/In_battle) the FIGHT command misses. The player wins by using ACT on each friend the boss has swallowed until that friend remembers who they are. [Mysterium](https://officialgamerules.org/game-rules/mysterium/) ends the same way in board game form. Every earlier round feeds one last shared vision and a single vote on the culprit. [Return of the Obra Dinn](https://www.godisageek.com/reviews/return-of-the-obra-dinn-review/) only confirms fates in sets of three, so guessing does not pay. This works because the final test asks whether players took the story in, and it needs no new rules. For four novices in under 30 minutes, the risk is that someone missed a clue and the group stalls. They need the knowledge in their hands as objects, and a medium who can nudge.

### 3.2 One accusation, one vote

Murder mystery parties end with every guest writing down an accusation, then the host reads the solution. Night of Mystery's [host guide](https://www.nightofmystery.com/?p=389499) calls that reveal a crowd favorite. Jubensha, the Chinese scripted murder format, ends the same way. Room Escape Artist's [write-up](https://roomescapeartist.com/2026/06/10/killer-storytelling-the-rise-of-jubensha/) says players vote and "the host then reveals the truth". [One Night Ultimate Werewolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultimate_Werewolf) cut Werewolf down to one night, ten minutes of talk and one vote, with nobody eliminated. It works because everyone acts at the same instant and the answer is plainly right or wrong. The risk here is thin evidence. With four players and 25 minutes of play, a vote can be a coin flip, and a wrong one lands on a friend.

### 3.3 The traitor is revealed, and someone gets a last move

[Betrayal at House on the Hill](https://babel.speldesign.uu.se/?p=6989) names a traitor halfway through and sends that player out of the room to read separate rules. Fans love it for the stories and admit it is unbalanced. One [fan's defense](https://waytoomany.games/2020/09/14/my-favorite-board-game-is-a-disaster-and-i-dont-care/) of it says "you can't play to win". [Avalon](https://officialgamerules.org/game-rules/the-resistance-avalon/) does the last move better. When the good side has won, the Assassin gets one guess at who Merlin is and can steal the game. [Dead of Winter](https://www.spritesanddice.com/reviews/dead-winter-review-making-survival-matter/) gives the betrayer a private goal and lets the group vote to exile a suspect, who is sometimes innocent. The reveal recasts everything that came before, and the last move keeps the result open to the final second. The risk is rules load. A traitor who has to learn a second rulebook mid-game is the weak spot of Betrayal. A novice handed a traitor card may freeze or overplay it.

### 3.4 A final choice or sacrifice

Burns wrote a second Room Escape Artist piece on [player choice](https://roomescapeartist.com/2025/07/20/escape-rooms-player-choice/). He lists the common versions in escape rooms: finish the mission or take the noble path, turn villain, or have one player stay behind so the rest go free. He says these choices often fall flat. Players are unsure of the story, the choice breaks the flow, and they start guessing which option has the better ending. He asks for a choice that is easy to understand, has real consequences and was foreshadowed, from a designer who has thought about what a stalled debate costs. When it works it works well. Reviewers of [The Signal](https://escapethereview.co.uk/?p=4675) were told afterward that teams split almost evenly on its final choice. Pyre frees one teammate per Liberation Rite and never has enough rites. Its director told [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/pyre-developer-explains-why-theres-no-best-ending-1797727879) "there is no best ending in Pyre". Nier: Automata's last ending [asks players](https://kotaku.com/theres-a-difficult-decision-at-the-end-of-nier-automat-1793071026) to delete their own save file to help strangers. A cost the players choose hits harder than a cost dealt to them. The risk for novices is a stalled debate with a line of guests waiting outside.

### 3.5 The dead keep playing

In [Blood on the Clocktower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_on_the_Clocktower), "dead players are not eliminated and can participate freely in discussions", and each keeps one vote for the rest of the game. In classic Werewolf the first players out sit and watch. The live version of this is Trapped in a Room with a Zombie. Page 20 of [Nicholson's survey](https://scottnicholson.com/pubs/erfacwhite.pdf) says a player the zombie touches "can no longer move around the room" but stays in the game. Nobody's night ends early, and the removed player still cares how it ends. The risk is that every extra role is another rule to teach. A removed player's job has to fit in one sentence.

### 3.6 Split the group, then bring it back

At the end of The Man From Beyond, a performer's hand "takes one player away from the group" for a private scene "while the other guests are cast in new roles for the finale", according to [No Proscenium](https://www.noproscenium.com/houstons-the-man-from-beyond-is-otherworldly-fun-review/). [Sleep No More](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleep_No_More_%282011_play%29) had one-on-ones, where an actor picks one guest, gives them a task or whispers to them, and leads them somewhere private. Then She Fell took 15 people a night and kept them "often split into ones and twos", as No Proscenium's [farewell piece](https://www.noproscenium.com/celebrating-then-she-fell/) puts it. The Willows gives one guest per show a featured role. Its [reviewer](https://www.noproscenium.com/house-of-secrets-a-night-with-the-willows-review/) warns that everyone else will be "wondering about that one-on-one for days". Being singled out is the strongest memory this kind of theater makes. The risk is the reunion. The Hex Room quote in section 2 is the warning. It also takes a performer out of the main room each time.

### 3.7 A clock that takes things away

Nicholson describes Apocalypse at Escape Canada on page 8 of [Ask Why](https://scottnicholson.com/pubs/askwhy.pdf). The game is 60 minutes, but the first room is only safe for 30. A team that has not reached the bunker by then has lost. In the zombie room the chain gets longer every five minutes. Ten Candles is a tabletop horror game lit by ten candles that go out one by one. The [Shut Up & Sit Down review](https://www.shutupandsitdown.com/review-ten-candles) notes that the room gets darker as the story does. A countdown you can see and touch tells players what is coming without a word of rules. The risk is a hard stop that cuts a novice group off mid-sentence. The clock also has to be readable by a player who is blind or deaf at the time, so it needs light and a voice.

### 3.8 An ending performed on the player, by name

When you lose in [Inscryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscryption), the dealer photographs you and turns you into a "death card" that you name. The card turns up in later runs. At the end of Sleep No More, actors took a guest's hand, pulled them to the bar and whispered "a few words before departing". Darkfield's [Séance](https://theplusones.com/sydney/2023/06/02/darkfield-a-review-of-flight-and-seance/) is 20 minutes in total darkness with hands on a table and a medium's voice in your ear. Losing becomes a scene that happens to you personally, which makes losing worth doing. The risk is touch and darkness with novices. It needs a consent line at the door and a stop word, and the performer needs a rehearsed path.

### 3.9 Chance, weighted by what you did

Just a Little Lovin' is a Nordic larp about the AIDS years. Each act ends with a "lottery of death". Annika Waern's [account](https://annikawaern.wordpress.com/2012/06/16/just-a-little-lovin-and-techniques-for-telling-stories-in-larp/) describes two angels in black handing out tickets. "Depending on your risk level, you put one to five tickets with your name on into the lottery", and the ten people called learn their fate in a side room. [Until Dawn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Until_Dawn) lets any of its eight characters live or die depending on play. The count is never scripted, and players accept the draw because they bought their own tickets. The risk is that a group that played well still loses someone and calls it unfair. Skill needs a way to cut the draw to zero.

### 3.10 Graded endings and partial wins

Nicholson's 2015 survey found an average escape rate of 41 percent, with wide spread between rooms. That is on page 22. On pages 27 and 28 he describes Memori, a library game with several endings, "each of which was satisfactory", where players saw the ending that matched how many puzzles they solved. Pyre and Until Dawn both end on a count of who made it. A result that is a number gives groups something to compare at the party. The risk is the opposite of harshness. If every ending feels fine, winning stops mattering. Burns names the moment a host says "either choice is fine" as the thing that kills a choice.

### 3.11 The complicity reveal

Brenda Romero's board game [Train](https://gamesbeat.com/brenda-romero-train-board-game-holocaust) has players load yellow pegs into boxcars as efficiently as they can. Then a card names the destination, Auschwitz. She wanted players to feel "complicity". Walt Williams gave a [GDC talk in 2013](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-i-spec-ops-the-line-i-contextualizes-violence-through-story) on how Spec Ops: The Line wraps a shooter's one verb in a story that makes the player answer for it. This pattern suits the mother. She needs lives willingly given, so the ending can count back every yes the players gave her. On page 11 of Ask Why, Nicholson passes on Evan Skolnick's test for a surprise. It has to be set up beforehand and "make sense afterwards to the players". The risk is that players never noticed they were agreeing. Then the reveal reads as a cheat. Each yes has to leave a mark they can see.

### 3.12 The beat after the climax

Burns asks for a denouement, a short stretch after the climax that shows what changed. The Man From Beyond ends with "a short play" and "an in-universe memento", and [ARGNet](https://www.argn.com/2017/11/giving_the_escape_room_stories_room_to_breathe/) reports that its makers publish no success rate. Until Dawn runs its credits over police interviews with whoever lived. Megagames end with a debrief for the whole room, usually about half an hour. A [Megagame Makers guide](https://megagamemaker.com/2017/01/05/) gives the aim as "to allow players to get a sense of some of the unfolding narrative that they would never know about otherwise". Page 24 of Nicholson's survey says 73 percent of escape room facilities schedule a debrief. The [peak-end rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak%E2%80%93end_rule) says memory keeps the most intense moment of an experience and its end. The risk here is time. With five groups back to back, this beat gets 60 to 90 seconds and has to happen outside the last room so reset can start.

## 4. Design principles from the writing

These are the rules I leaned on for the proposals.

- Ask why. Nicholson's [Ask Why](https://scottnicholson.com/pubs/askwhy.pdf) paper says every puzzle, task and prop should have a reason in the story for being there. For the finale, the last task should be the thing the story needs done.
- Give players a role and let their actions pick the ending. On pages 11, 14 and 15 Nicholson calls rooms that do this "hypernarrative". He says "a simple way" to get there is "multiple endings to the game based upon the players' actions".
- A surprise has to be set up, and it has to make sense afterward. This is Skolnick's test from 3.11.
- Have a climax, then show what it changed. The first half is in Room Escape Artist's [dos and don'ts](https://roomescapeartist.com/2016/07/17/the-room-escape-artist-dos-and-donts/). The second half is Burns on denouement.
- A final choice has to be simple, consequential and foreshadowed, and it has to survive a group that cannot agree. That is Burns again.
- When a split group comes back together, the reunion needs its own job. The Hex Room review is the cautionary case.
- Tell everyone the rules together, in the fiction, before the clock starts. ARGNet credits The Man From Beyond's theater opening for the fact that "every player is aware of what they're doing".
- Give a shy player a prop and a task. Jon Braver of Delusion told the [Room Escape Artist podcast](https://roomescapeartist.com/2021/09/28/s2e8-cinematic-delusions-jon-braver/) that this is one of the best ways to get reluctant people to join in.
- Let the mechanic carry the meaning. Romero's series is called The Mechanic is the Message. What Remains of Edith Finch gives each death its own way to play, the subject of Ian Dallas's GDC 2018 talk "Weaving 13 Prototypes into 1 Game".
- Do not promise a best ending you cannot give honestly. Kasavin on Pyre said "We really didn't want to be dishonest with the choices that players made along the way."
- Taking a sense away works as a core rule for a group of four. [Nyctophobia](https://www.avclub.com/nyctophobia-review) is a published board game where up to four players wear blackout glasses and play by touch while one sighted player hunts them.

One principle I could only confirm from a search summary. Pawel Ochocki gave a GDC talk on the endings of Cyberpunk 2077: Phantom Liberty with the line "endings are about earned resolutions, not wish fulfillment". Both pages that report it refused my fetch, so treat the wording as unverified.

## 5. Five finale proposals for this game

All five share a frame. I am assuming one host plays the sincere medium in the room and the other plays the mother. Each player is tied to one child from the start by the keepsake they are handed. The last room has four name plates and four candles. Curses still on at the finale stay on.

All five also need the same line just before the finale. The medium works out what "bring them back" means and says the rule in plain words. A draft: "She does not want them found. She wants them replaced. Four were taken, so four must be given, and she can only keep what you hand her." Without that line no ending will make sense to players.

### 5.1 The roll call

The medium turns the sand timer. The mother stands in the doorway and calls her children home, eldest first. "Deliverance." The group has to answer for him. Someone puts his keepsake on his name plate and someone says out loud what happened to him. If the answer is the truth, the medium snuffs his candle. He is at rest, and the player who carried his keepsake is safe. Then she calls "Waitstill." Four names in about four minutes. Remember, the six-year-old, comes last.

Only the third layer of the history works as an answer. The official record, that she was tried and there was a fire, does nothing. Her version, that her husband did it, gets a "Yes" from her and the candle stays lit. The answer that works names her part, something like "You sewed the letters into his coat and sent him out. The soldiers took his hands." Curses bite here. A mute player cannot give the answer, a blind one cannot find the plate, a handless one cannot place the keepsake, and a deaf one cannot hear which name she called. The healer's one cure is worth saving for this. A keepsake given up earlier is pinned to her apron. That player has to walk up to her, take it back and say their own name, and the curse she lifted for it comes back. When the sand runs out she takes each player whose child still has a lit candle. She uses the child's name and leads them out by the hand. The Informer's card says they go free if two or more candles are still lit. Their tools are small: answer first with her version, fumble a keepsake, talk over a helper. As she leaves she puts a coin in the Informer's hand, so the group finds out who it was whether the Informer won or lost.

The mother calls the names, judges the answers and does the taking. Her judging rule is short. Accept any answer that names the child's job and says the mother sent them. The medium keeps the timer and the candles and gives hints in character, such as "That is her story, not his." Build is four name plates, four LED candles, an apron and one card per child carrying the true account. Players earn those cards in the earlier games, so the group can read the truth off a card when memory fails. That is an evening or two of work. Reset is about two minutes.

### 5.2 The four chairs

This one answers what happens to a player who is taken mid-game. They do not go back to the party. The mother walks them to the last room and sits them in that child's small chair, with the child's cap on and a candle in their hands. She whispers one rule: "Sit still. Answer to nothing but your own name." As they are taken, they hand their keepsake to a friend. When the survivors reach the last room they find their friends sitting there as her children. The last five minutes are the rescue. For each seated friend, the group lays the child's keepsake in their lap, tells the child the truth of the death, and then calls the friend by their real name. The friend stands, leaves the cap on the chair and joins in.

When the sand runs out, whoever is still seated stays, and the door closes on them. The count is whoever walks out. Each rescue gives the group another pair of hands and maybe a missing sense, so the order matters. A group short of a voice should free a talker first. Curses and the three layers work as in the roll call, and her version of a death frees nobody. The keepsake is the means of rescue, which is a plain reason to keep it. The Informer goes free if two or more chairs are still full, and can stall or push her version. Both sets of names get used. The child's name releases the child and the real name wakes the friend, so the register at the door pays off. A group that lost nobody finds four empty chairs, and the mother starts calling names. That is the roll call. The two proposals are one rite.

The mother leaves the main game for under a minute at each taking, and the medium covers. A seated player may wait five to ten minutes alone, listening through the door, which suits some guests and not others. Build is four child chairs or stools, four caps or shawls and LED candles, mostly a thrift store trip. Reset is about two minutes. I should be straight that this is a cousin of the reappearance idea the captain called clunky. The difference is that nothing is sprung on anyone and the returning player has no rules to learn. They are the prize, sitting in plain sight.

### 5.3 Buttons in the bag

Each player gets a colour and five cloth buttons at the door, since the mother hid her notes in coat buttons. Her sewing bag sits on the table all night. Every curse a player takes puts one of their buttons in the bag. Giving her a keepsake puts in two. Everyone can watch the bag fill. In the last five minutes the group does the name plate task from the roll call, where only the true account of each death counts. Then she draws. She pulls one button for each child still not at rest, holds it up, says the child's name and then reads the owner's real name from the register. That player is hers.

If all four children are at rest there is no draw and everyone walks out. Otherwise chance decides, weighted by how much each player gave her through the night. A player with no buttons in the bag cannot be drawn. If the same colour comes up twice the second draw is wasted, since she already has that player. So the number taken can land anywhere from zero to four and nobody can call it in advance. This is the proposal that best answers the wish for chance in the count, and it gives the keepsake a price at the moment of temptation. The Informer carries spare buttons in the other three colours and slips them into the bag when nobody is looking. An Informer caught in the act puts all five of their own in. Before the draw the bag is tipped out and counted, and a count higher than the group remembers tells them someone fed it.

The mother draws slowly and the medium keeps the bag honest. Build is a drawstring bag, about 30 buttons in four colours and the register. Reset is a minute of sorting. The risk is a group that played well and still loses a friend to one bad draw. Letting a perfect final task cancel the draw is the fix.

### 5.4 Closing the circle

Everyone goes back to the séance table. Hands flat, one candle in front of each player. The medium says a séance has to be closed or the spirit stays, and each sitter must send their own child on. The sitter puts the keepsake in the middle of the table and says the child's name and the truth of the death. The medium snuffs that candle, and the room gets darker each time. A player with no keepsake has nothing to return. Their candle stays lit, and in a dark room it marks them. A friend can slide their own keepsake across to cover them, but then the friend has nothing. That is a life willingly given, which is what she has waited for all night. When the sand runs out she blows out what is left. Ten seconds of full dark, a hand on a shoulder, and the light comes back on empty chairs.

Anyone whose child is not at rest at lights out is taken in the dark, and the survivors learn the count by looking around the table. Most of that is decided by keepsakes kept or lost earlier, so it needs live moves to avoid being the tally in disguise. The slide across the table is one. A second is that she will trade a keepsake back to anyone who tells her the third layer of the history to her face. A third is that a mute sitter needs someone to speak the rite for them. The Informer wants two empty chairs. In the dark she leaves a coin on the table in front of the Informer, and when the light returns the coin tells the story.

The medium runs the rite and the light. The mother moves in the dark and needs a clear, rehearsed route. Build is close to nothing: the table, LED candles, a blacked out room and a coin. Reset is a minute. This is the scariest of the five and the least like a game. It needs a consent line at the door and a stop word.

### 5.5 The Informer's last word

The last thing players learn is that one of her own ring sold her. She turns it on them. "It has happened again. One of you has carried to me all night." The medium turns a two-minute timer and the group talks. On her count of three everyone points at once. If most fingers find the Informer, she offers them, out loud, the deal the first informer took: "Give me one of them and walk out." The Informer can name a friend and leave, or refuse and hand back whatever they passed to her. If the fingers miss, she takes the player they accused, calling them by their child's name, and the real Informer keeps their cover. A short name plate rite settles the rest.

The group wins if the children rest. The Informer wins by walking out with two or more players taken. An Informer who refuses the deal wins with the group. Keepsakes are the evidence, since the argument is about who lost theirs and when. Curses make the debate funny and hard, with one player who cannot hear it and another who cannot speak in it. The vote replays the third layer of the history, and the Informer's choice is the one the first informer got wrong. The first informer needs a name for that to land, and players need to have learned it.

The mother runs the vote and the medium stays out of it. Nothing new to build and nothing to reset. The risks are the ones in 3.2 and 3.3. Four players is thin for social deduction. Each loyal player is guessing one in three on little evidence, a wrong vote sends a friend out on a guess, and a two-minute debate can stall. I would keep the Informer in the game but not hang the finale on a vote.

## 6. Ranked recommendation

1. **The roll call.** It fixes all five gaps in section 2. She is in the room as the opponent. The result stays open until the sand is gone. The truth is the key, so the three layers of history pay off as three possible answers. Curses, the healer and the keepsakes all matter in the last five minutes. The count runs from zero to four and comes from play. Players can state the rule in one sentence: lies hold the children here and the truth lets them go. It is cheap and resets in two minutes.
2. **The four chairs.** I rank it second only because it depends on a decision that is still open, whether players are taken during the game. If they are, build the roll call and the chairs together. They use the same rite and the same props, and the chairs give removed players a stake and survivors a reason to care.
3. **Buttons in the bag.** The best answer to chance in the count and to why anyone keeps a keepsake. I put it third because a draw decides the ending, and a finish won by skill feels more like winning a game. There is a clean blend if the captain wants real chance. Run the roll call, and let each child still unrested at the end be one draw from the bag. The roll call sets how many are taken and the bag sets who.
4. **Closing the circle.** The strongest piece of theater and the weakest game. Its last ten seconds are worth stealing. Ending the roll call with a blackout and empty chairs would make the taking land harder than leading people out in the light.
5. **The Informer's last word.** Weakest for four novices. Keep the Informer as a saboteur with a private goal and a coin at the end, and skip the vote.

Here is the recommended finale as a run sheet, for a 25-minute game.

- Minute 20. The last game ends with the truth about the couriers and the informer. The medium says the rule line.
- Minute 21. The group enters the last room. Four plates, four candles, and any taken friends in their chairs. The medium turns the timer.
- Minutes 21 to 25. Roll call and rescues. The mother calls, the group answers, candles go out.
- Minute 25. Sand out. She takes whoever is left, by the child's name. She puts the coin in the Informer's hand.
- Minute 26. The medium walks the survivors out and chalks the result on a slate by the door: "Entered 4. Returned 2." Taken players come out a minute later wearing the child's name pinned to them and keep it on for the rest of the party.

The slate and the name tags are the beat after the climax. Five groups can compare numbers all night, and the tags are a running joke that is also a little grim. When the fifth group is through, the hosts can name each group's Informer in front of everyone. That is the megagame debrief at party size, and it is where the traitor stories get told.

## 7. Open questions for the captain

These ride on backlog task `halloween-2026-review`, which was already waiting on the captain for the ending, the lure and tuning. I added the finale choice to it and kept its earlier questions word for word.

1. Are players taken during the game, or only at the end? The September 30 notes record it as decided. The October 1 session turned it back into a maybe. The four chairs only matter if the answer is during.
2. If a player is taken, do they wait in the last room in costume, or go back to the party?
3. Is each player tied to one child from the first minute by their keepsake? All five proposals assume yes.
4. Do you want literal chance in the count, as in the bag of buttons, or is a count that comes from play enough?
5. Can the Informer win, and does she keep her promise to them? I assumed yes to both, since she never lies about what she wants. Is the Informer a drawn card or a volunteer?
6. How often should a group get all four out? Escape rooms in Nicholson's 2015 survey averaged about 41 percent. I would aim for one group in five, with most groups losing one or two.
7. Does the mother appear in person in the finale, and may she take a player's hand? That needs a consent line at the door and a stop word.
8. Who plays whom? I assumed one host is the medium and one is the mother. If the mediums are extra people, the hosts are freer in the finale.
9. Real flame or LED candles? With blindfolded players I recommend LED.
10. How much do you mind the first group telling the fifth what happens? The roll call survives it better than a twist does, since knowing the format does not beat the clock. The truths themselves could leak.
11. For the surname, Graves makes the name plates do double duty. A roll call also favours names that sound good shouted across a room.
